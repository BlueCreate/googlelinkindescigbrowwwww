population distribution	The pattern of people scattered over an area.
population density	The number of people within a given area.
ecumene	The habitable parts of the world. Example: Along fertile rivers and plains.
arithmetic population density	A measure of the total number of people within a given area divided by the total land area. Example: Egypt has 182 persons per square mile, while Japan has 879.
physiologic population density	A measure of the number of people per arable (farmable) land. Example: Egypt has 9,064 persons per arable land, while Japan has 7,944.
agricultural population density	A measure of the number of farmers per arable land.
carrying capacity	The number of people an area can sustain without critically straining its resources.
population pyramid	A visual representation of a population in terms of age and sex. It provides an indication of the dependency ratio, assesses population growth/decline, and predicts markets for goods/services. Example: Wide base represents rapid growth; rectangular shape represents stable growth.
total fertility rate (TFR)	The average number of children a woman will have during her childbearing years (15–49).
birth rate	The number of live births in a single year for every 1,000 people in a population.
replacement fertility level	The fertility rate needed to maintain population levels (2.1 to account for infant/childhood mortality and childless women).
mortality (death) rate	The number of deaths in a single year for every 1,000 people in a population.
infant mortality rate	The number of deaths during the 1st year of life per 1,000 live births.
child mortality rate	The number of deaths between the ages of 1 and 5 per 1,000 children.
maternal mortality rate	The number of deaths during or shortly after childbirth per 100,000 live births.
migration	A movement to a new locale that involves a degree of permanence.
emigration (out migration)	The movement of people out of a particular place.
immigration (in migration)	The movement of people into a particular place.
transnational migration	Migration that occurs across national boundaries.
internal migration	Migration that occurs within national boundaries. Example: Moving from the northern United States to the south.
natural increase	The birth rate minus the death rate.
population doubling time	The length of time required for a population to double in size. Example: Countries with a 1% growth rate take approximately 70 years to double; at the current global rate, it takes about 54 years.
Demographic Transition Model	A model that describes the relationship between population change and the development of a country over time. Example: Stage 2 represents rapid population growth (e.g., Afghanistan), while Stage 5 represents population decline (e.g., Japan).
epidemiologic transition (mortality revolution)	The sudden increase in population due to medical innovations causing a sharp decrease in the death rate.
population explosion	The very great and continuing increase in the human population in modern times. Example: An increase of more than 5.5 billion people in the past 200 years.
pro-natalist population policies	Government policies that provide incentives for women to have children, typically in countries with declining populations. Example: Policies in Japan, Singapore, Denmark, Germany, and Italy.
anti-natalist population policies	Government policies that encourage couples to limit the number of children they have. Example: China's One-Child Policy, family planning in Iran, or sterilization programs in India.
immigration policies	Policies that address and regulate the movement of persons across international borders.
Ravenstein’s laws of migration	A set of 1885 migration tendencies stating that most migrants move short distances, long-distance migrants choose big cities, flows generate counter-flows, rural populations migrate more than urban, and economic reasons dominate.
population aging	An increasing median age in the population determined by declining birth rates, dropping death rates, and rising life expectancy. Example: The "graying" of America or Japan.
life expectancy	The average number of years a newborn infant can expect to live.
aging index	The number of people age 65 and older per 100 children ages 0–14. Example: Europe has 263 older people per 100 children, while Africa has 37.
dependency ratio	A measure of the economic impact of younger and older cohorts on the economically productive members of a population.
pull factors	Positive characteristics that attract a person to a new place. Example: Job opportunities, religious freedom, or a desirable climate.
push factors	Negative characteristics that make a person want to leave their current place. Example: Poverty, religious persecution, or political instability.
intervening opportunity	The presence of a nearer opportunity that greatly diminishes the attractiveness of sites farther away. Example: Finding a higher-paying job while en route to a original migration destination.
intervening obstacle	An event or obstacle (environmental, economic, political, or cultural) that discourages or prevents people from migrating. Example: High travel costs, physical distance, language barriers, or strict laws.
genocide	A premeditated, organized effort to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Example: The Holocaust, Rwanda, and Cambodia.
ethnic cleansing	The effort to rid a country or region of a particular ethnicity through forced migration or genocide. Example: The conflict in the former Yugoslavia.
forced migration	A migration movement in which an individual moves against his or her will due to cultural or environmental factors. Example: The North Atlantic slave trade or refugees fleeing conflict.
refugee	An individual protected by law who crosses national boundaries to seek safety from armed conflict or persecution. Example: Syrians fleeing civil war or Rohingya fleeing persecution in Myanmar.
asylum seeker	An individual who flees their home country and applies for protection, but whose request for sanctuary has not yet been processed.
internally displaced person (IDP)	An individual who is forced to leave their home due to conflict, human rights abuses, or catastrophes but does not cross international borders. Example: New Orleans flood victims or Syrians fleeing to other parts of Syria.
voluntary migration	A movement in which an individual chooses to migrate, typically based on a evaluation of push and pull factors. Example: Europeans moving to North America.
transhumance	A seasonal periodic movement of pastoral nomads who move livestock between summer and winter pastures.
chain migration	The migration of people to a specific location because relatives or members of the same nationality previously migrated there.
step migration	A migration process to a distant destination that occurs in stages or steps. Example: Moving from a farm to a nearby village, then to a town, and finally to a large city.
guest workers	A person granted temporary legal permission to work in another country, often providing migrant labor.
rural to urban migration	The permanent movement of people from the countryside to cities, causing the expansion of urban areas.
Rust Belt	An area in the upper Midwest of the United States that was once an industrial powerhouse but lost much of its economic base to other regions and countries.
Sun Belt	The states in the American South and West Coast that saw a massive influx of internal migrants beginning in the 1960s and 1970s, altering the balance of political and economic power. Example: California, Florida, and Texas.
